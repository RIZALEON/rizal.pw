Are We Alone?

@ChaseRizal · 2025-01-15 · article

Introduction: Boxes

In the beginning of our academic journeys, we are told repeatedly to draw within the lines, don’t go outside of the box, stay within the mainstream of academia, and don’t ask questions that aren’t in line with typical, normative paradigms. Shall we ever dare to ask . . . why? What is outside of the box? What will happen outside the lines? If we glimpse over the walls of the ivory tower, what will we bear witness too? As we advance in our studies and research, we are allotted more and more independence and liberty, especially when our findings are based on scientific or factual data.

I remember vividly even in my junior college days hitting many brick walls when presenting ideas and thoughts that at the time seemed commonsensical. Such as the similarities between the Indigenous Americas and the indigenous cultures of Asia, the Pacific, and beyond. I saw this architecturally with pyramids and mounds, and culturally with symbolism and motifs. One being the dragon or flying serpent which is found predominantly in almost every Asian and Indigenous American culture as a foundational element; we have Quetzalcoatl, Kukulkan, Naga, Bakunawa in the Philippines, Loong the Chinese dragon, and Tatsu from Indigenous Japan, just to name a few. Biologically both genetically and anatomically as Asians, Pacific Islanders and Indigenous Americans we are most similar in these categories than those from any other global region; in many instances, those similarities are indistinguishable on genetic and optical levels. While there is most definitely A shared origin and history, this seems to be one of those contentious and delicate topics to address within the box of academia.

The Indigenous Americans and Asians who mastered the antediluvian world, populated the Americas North to South, excelled in a multitude of sciences and technologies such as stone masonry, metallurgy, astronomy, language, and mathematics left erasable evidence of their ancient grandeur and genius in the archaeological record in places such as Teotihuacan, Machu Picchu , Tenochtitlan, Angkor wat, Sanxangdui, etc… there is so much more to the story than just the mainstream ideal that: 10 thousand years ago hunter-gatherers crossing ice bridges during the last ice age from eastern asia to the northwestern americas on foot populated the Americas which interestingly enough was the same narrative for how the Philippines was populated until recently when it was evidenced that Luzon is a true Archipelago formed by volcanic and tectonic activity sometime after the Jurrsasic period and was never connected to pangea or any other continental land mass, that is why no dinosaur fossils have ever been found in The Philippines, they went extinct before its creation.

As time went along, we discovered more archaeological and scientific data, and the dates continued to be pushed 2further back from 10 to 20 to 40, and now with the stone tools and butchered mastodon bones uncovered in San Diego,CA that have been dated back to 130,000kya, the evidence has become so monumental that everything that was regurgitated for the past 300 some odd years had to be completely retooled and rewritten. There was no longer a gatekeeper with one finger on their lips whispering shhhhhh!

Mainstream Science currently states the modern human became the sole survivor somewhere between 15-40 kya, and the “modern” homo sapiens only departed from Africa around 150 kya. So if we have Homo Erectus as the “paleoanthropologist postulates in the Philippines over 700kya, the Homo luzonensis which is the newest addition to the Hominid family found in another cave in the Kalinga province of northern Luzon radiocarbon dated to over 70 kya, and we have the most recent and extensive genetic study on indigenous Filipino population groups performed to date this past year that shows the highest level of living Denisovan DNA in the world in the Philippines Ayta, what exactly is going on? is it the Tsunami Surfing Cavemen theory that shall win the day? I highly doubt it.

This great nation of over 7000 islands just might hold all the answers to the mysteries of our past. Although unfortunately when viewing the world through the old antiquated academic spectacles, I see a world of uniformity, everyone's the same, science is omniscient, and there is nothing left to be discovered. As researchers, anthropologists, and academics, we have the ability to go out into the world to find, restore, and reveal these missing puzzle pieces so all can gaze upon the masterpiece we share with all who have come before and shall come after.

I am uncertain where this interest of mine as an individual researcher began whether it was the consistent elementary reiterations of “stay in the box,” the early college level manifestations of that same idea to “not ask questions outside the mainstream,” or my own personal journey of rediscovery which led me to trace my roots at 33 years old back to my motherland. Moving from California to The Philippines with nothing and not knowing anybody, I lived in the islands for over 5 years continuously, learning Tagalog, and growing roots in the fertile volcanic soil of my place of providence, without a green dollar to my name at that. I am not sure, but I can say that gaining the acceptance of my fellow Filipinos which I had strived for my entire life growing up, but had always been kept from, and to answer all the questions one has always had, answered. Was an extremely profound and powerful experience, worth more than any weight of gold for it is when we restore all that has been lost in the past and return it to the present, our futures have no bounds.

Body: Workshop of the Renacimiento

In Spanish, renacimiento means rebirth or revival which this pandemic has incubated for so many of us for better or worse. It did the same for me as it was the catalyst for my return to America and Academia. I felt as though, if there was ever a time for a self-update or upgrade it was now. Reyes used a similar moment he termed the “workshop of the Renacamiento” referencing that his book was completed during the fight for independence in the Philippines. I knew it would not be an easy return for a multitude of reasons, but mainly because I never well understood our social structures and systems there and was a fish out of water if you will. To potentially return to an ocean full of sharks, with aggressive and sharp ideologies, after the return upstream to my place of origin, the Philippines, would be uncomfortable for so many reasons. Although I knew it was a feat within my means, especially now with so many more tools in my toolbox, I walked through that door with the intention of having the greatest effect on everything and everyone I interacted with to change things for the greater good while at the same time holding closely the great importance of providing for my Filipino family who did not have that same opportunity to cross the ocean and pursue greater destinies. With and keeping true to my straightforward nature, I found America to be even more strange and confusing than when I had left back in 2015.

I remember the day in class vividly; it was biological anthropology, and we were asked to theorize and comment on the extinction of all other hominids and the fact that “modern” homo sapiens were the last ones standing. For whatever reason this pulled my heartstrings and set me into deep contemplation. What a shame, a tragedy. What could be the reason or cause of such an event? I thought. I remember hoping that not all those different from us were extinct, that they had not all been wiped from existence, and that just maybe some had survived. What a tragedy to be the last bear on the mountain, to go missing forever, extinguished from existence, never to be seen or heard from again. I went home flustered after class and resumed work translating the first Filipino ethnography on Indigenous Filipinos performed by a Filipino Anthropologist during the late 1900’s from Spanish to English. I was approaching “Chapter 10: Ayta lords of The Mountains.”

I Had just left a few days ago from visiting and speaking with elders and youngsters alike at The Ati-Borakaynon village about some of the details and facts that follow. My analysis began to merge the past, the present, and the future, to find connections between the science of technology and the results contained within the foundation research article based upon genetics, which now seem to have fallen in lockstep with one another. Evidenced clearly from the first pages of Chapter 10 about the Aytas (Ati): “First we will explore the theogony of The Ayta who seem to be the aborigines or most ancient and original people throughout the whole world, perhaps they are the missing link many have sought after between man and its Ancient ancestors.”4 Written during the 1890s by Isabelo Reyes, one of the enlightened Filipinos of the era educated in the renowned Universities of Europe, this was a surprising commentary to some. But what if we, like Reyes, ask the question no one dares to ask? Could there be? Would there? How did the survivors against all odds remain? Even if it is just a small percentage of genetic remnants found within modern populations, it is that not enough to say you are here with us, you have survived which brings us to the most recent genetic science within mainstream academia, A 2021 genetic study published in the Biology Journal found that: “Philippine Ayta to possess the highest level of Denisovan ancestry in the world—30%–40% greater than that of Australopapuans consistent with an independent admixture event into Negritos from Denisovans.

The Philippine archipelago is thus likely inhabited by multiple archaic groups prior to the arrival of modern humans.”5 Which shockingly enough corroborates this ancient ethnography, and my current conversations alike with the Ayta, with science bestowing upon them the great honor of having the highest level of living Denisovan DNA. Now it seems undeniable that the Ayta are the most ancient people of the Philippines, this was echoed by Chief Justo whom I was lucky enough to converse with and learn from. She assured me that the Ati, which is their traditional spelling, are the first Filipinos and were here since the beginning. They are our ancestors.

Conclusion: Manifest Bagong Tadhana

In Tagalog Tadhana means destiny and Bago means new so to manifest a new destiny (bagong tadhana) is something we can all do at any time at any place if we so choose we have the ability to set down our sails and chart a new course set by our determination, and once you have that tool to utilize it is an extremely powerful one, which is why when I was reminded recently in a recent project by one of my fellow cohorts in the Maps program at USF and also a fellow Filipino due to her well written annotated bibliography in which the Kalinga Indigenous peoples, their traditional tattooing and Apo Whang od herself were mentioned of a long held desire i've had to meet Apo Whang Od one of the legendary mambabatoks which is the title given to the Indegenous Tattoo masters in which she is still participating at 105 years old and visiting the over 2,000 old Banaue rice terraces of the same province Kalinga while at the same time performing some preliminary ethnographic research, It was an opportunity I had to pursue especially since I was already here in The Philippines.

It was a very profound experience. Which strengthened my resolve and interest in researching, supporting, and championing the causes whenever possible of the many great Indigenous peoples of The Philippines. One thing that really stood out was the great importance of Anthropology, Ethnographies and academia in general to be present and supportive among the Indigenous peoples of the Philippines not just for us to learn from them but for us to advocate, assist and 8promote whenever possible, for example the Ayta Boracaynon needed advocacy to gain supporters to build a barracks and help develop self sustainable infrastructure and renewable energy production. The Kalinga But-But also would benefit greatly from the promotion of their numerous other great young up and coming mambabatoks and village access to free wifi.

All had so many great teachings to offer and elements to be studied. I hope we can find some balance in Academia of reciprocity of not just taking knowledge but giving something back to those that we gain from. With that being said I hope to be able to return and perform more in- depth and entrenched research over much longer periods of time when possible. This comingling of the past, present, and future, of Indigenous Filipino wisdom, modern science, and future aspirations informs the holistic anthropological approach I believe in and utilize.

Together we must turn academia’s direction towards the Pacific, as it is here in this great nation of over 7,000 islands and beyond, that many wonders and mysteries are yet to be uncovered. The surface is awaiting our curious hands to reach down and dust off the sands of time to reveal our world is filled with wonders and revelations beyond our expectations. It is often said that behind every legend and every myth there is truth at its foundation; in this particular case of the Ayta that ideal has proven to be true. I have the greatest hope and desire to draw attention, gather support, and build momentum with the steadfast intent of restoring, preserving, promoting, and protecting the histories, cultures, languages, and legends of this great island nation, The Philippines.
