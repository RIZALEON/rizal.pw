Living Legends

@ChaseRizal · 2024-11-10 · article

The Bridgewalker

Honoring Tradition and Legends

“.  . . to foretell the destiny of a nation, it is necessary to open the book that tells of her past.” - Dr. Jose Rizal

I begin with a question: Tell me the name of one Living Legend who walks amongst you? And by living legend I don't mean pop icon, famous movie star, or crafty politician, but some great manifestation of an individual in modern times who generates a great pride or point of providence for a nation, cultural or ethnic group, who stands regally as a guardian of a great ideal or vision. Who has held some pillar of civilization against all odds. Well? . . .  It is very difficult to do so off the top of one's head. Am I correct?

These legends link us to our past, can ground us to the present, and fuel our futures with their sacrifice and wisdom. This reverence and admiration is one I've always held for Indigenous peoples and causes. This magnetism towards and from these individuals and groups of torchbearers keeping the stories and cultures alive through the storms of modernity have held my attention and captivated my imagination as far back as I can remember. This has inspired me to be a Bridgewalker and to cross those dangerous bridges and walk those cliffs and trails up windy mountains, to receive the torch of the past from these living legends, these prolific light bearers, who walk amongst us to carry the flame of wisdom back across the bridge to us, helping us to connects the past, present and future.

I started with a question, and in the Philippines, this can be answered without contemplation: Apo Whang Od, A 105-year-old living legend from the Indigenous Kalingan ButBut tribe is a traditional Mambabatok—a chosen individual who performs traditional Filipino tattooing—a practice which has great power, meaning, and symbolism from pre-colonial times to present. Until this very day, she performs traditional Filipino tattooing day in and day out for pilgrims who journey over vast distances to meet, experience, and receive the blessing of this great Indigenous light bearer and living legend. Apo Whang Od is the reason why I decided that the people, places, and cultures will be my primary source, and I will simply cross bridges and carry back the blessings they've given.

While we can gain so much knowledge and insight by reading books, histories, diaries, and journals, when we go and sit down across from those who sit in the places that we have read about, this inspires our thoughts and imaginations. As we break bread, converse with them, we inhale the aromas of the provinces or town markets and learn from their ideals and perspectives This is the true essence of any primary source.

It was at the beginning of this semester after reading Eugenie Mamuyac’s annotated bibliography in which she utilized as one of her citations tapping ink, tattooing identities I was inspired as both in her writing and the article itself Apo Whang Od and the butbut tribe of Kalinga were central points. I thought to myself I've always wanted to meet Apo Whang od, experience the ceremony, and receive her blessing. I said to myself I am here now in the Philippines, and I was overcome with the feeling of a thought: “I must go now”,  for none of us know the day or time in which our hopes and dreams may no longer be within our grasp. The next day I began the long and treacherous journey to the mountain province of Kalinga to sit down with and hold the hand of my primary source, the great Indigenous Filipinos of Kalinga and the Legend herself Apo Whang Od.

Transmission of Knowledge to Younger Generations

As I read Eugenie Mamuyac’s piece and the text, they both had the heart shattering pronouncement that Apo Whang Od is the last traditional Filipino Mambabatok or traditional tattooist due to the decline of this practice and past negative connotations placed upon Indigenous traditions in the Philippines. This made it even more critical for me to reach this last Mambabatok before this great piece of pre-colonial Filipino heritage was extinguished. As I made it to Kalinga and then up the windy mountain trail to the top where the Butbut village was located, and passed many abodes to where I would be staying, I found to my amazement that since the writing of the cited journal article and because of this one remaining legendary mambabatok whom had gained international fanfare, the interest in continuing the tradition had inspired an entire new generation of Mambabatoks.

There were young Mambabatoks everywhere tattooing pilgrims with traditional symbols and designs of the Kalinga province before moving on to the next stage which was their meeting with Apo Whang Od and the ceremony of the three dots. This experience to see that this one 105 year old living legend had single handedly resurrected an entire cultural element and ritual while at the same time inspiring an entire generation to follow in her footsteps and carry on the legacies of our ancestors, not just with tattooing but teaching the traditional butbut language in local schools, was absolutely awe inspiring and heartwarming in contrast to what i was expecting to find.

Manifesting Visions & Monetizing Missions

I have always felt a close kinship with the Indigenous cultures and peoples. I have found a reoccurring truth that we most certainly share similar obstacles navigating a modern interconnected world of capitalism and the ability to monetize interests,cultural arts, crafts, pursuits, and projects that are not traditionally meant to be assigned a monetary value is very difficult puzzle to piece together,  although the ability to do just that may be one of the tools utilized in helping restore, preserve, and promote Indigenous cultures and languages before they are erased.  It is with this shared obstacle that my research may be able to intersect with the desires and struggles of The Indigenous Filipino. A few weeks ago I visited the Ati Boracaynon village in the western Visayas which is one of the many villages of the Ayta tribe that are found throughout the Philippines. They are resoundingly acknowledged as the first Filipinos, as they were here since the beginning as it was relayed to me by them. Many Filipinos and scholars alike have a great reverence for the Aeta and consider them to be our living ancestors and as in all ancestors we can learn a great deal. During my visit this struggle with integration was relayed to me in many different ways. They would much rather spend their time with the arts, creating, gardening, boating, fishing, etc…

They have found modernization to be a big obstacle in preserving their culture even day to day as they have to worry about paying the electric and water bill or it will be disconnected,which in my humble perspective indigenous groups should not be bothered with such ridiculous machinations as to the hustle and bustle 9 to 5 pay your bills or else ideologies, I find these quirks of our modernity unfortunate but in response the Aeta have many great ideals to remedy such matters, for example they would like to build a dormitory to house guests and visitors to the island as an extra revenue stream, they want to restore their fish farm, and gather funding for rehabilitating their garden and lagoon all of which of course needs … funding!

While here in the Philippines, I found that each particular Indigenous group has their own strengths and obstacles when it comes to natural resources, local economies, tourism/trade, and associated issues. The first important factor is access to the basics for us to have success in this modern interconnected world of online connections and commerce. Here, in the Third World, we need to have water, electricity, Wifi, and gadgets. pillars if you will for the ascension of the Indigenous Filipino. If we could help develop and then deliver clean renewable self sustainable systems such as solar, desalination tech, and Satellite Wifi such as starlink they would have a great foundation to rebuild and restore their cultures from which is critical, as not every tribe has individuals as world renowned as Whang Od to help in the restoration process. I believe tech can help crowdfunding and fundraising could be utilized as a great medium to begin projects and get boots on the ground to establish a more intricate and in-depth sustainable economic model for any particular tribe, village, culture, or indigenous group found in any of the 7,000 plus islands that make up this great nation of The Philippines.
